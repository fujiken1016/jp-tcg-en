How Japanese TCG Lotteries Actually Work

Written from inside Japan. No affiliate links on this page.

If you collect Pokémon, One Piece, Yu-Gi-Oh!, Lorcana or MTG and you have ever wondered why Japanese sealed product is either at retail or at triple retail with nothing in between — this is the missing piece. Most high-demand Japanese product is not sold first-come. It is sold by lottery, and the lottery has rules that are almost never written down in English.

1. What a Japanese lottery actually is

The Japanese word is 抽選 (chūsen). It is not a raffle you pay to enter, and it is not a preorder queue. It is a free application window: you apply, and later you are told whether you have earned the right to buy the item at its normal retail price.

Three things follow from that, and all three surprise people:

  • Applying costs nothing and losing costs nothing. There is no downside to entering every window you are eligible for. Japanese collectors enter dozens.
  • Winning is an obligation, not a prize. You are expected to complete the purchase, and for in-store lotteries, to physically collect within a stated window — often only a few days.
  • The price is retail. The lottery exists precisely because retail is far below the secondary market. That gap is the whole reason the system exists.

Each retailer runs its own window, on its own schedule, with its own account requirements. There is no central calendar. That fragmentation — not the odds — is the real barrier.

2. Where the lotteries run

Japanese sealed product does not funnel through one or two channels the way it tends to in the US. In practice the windows are spread across at least four different kinds of seller, and each kind behaves differently.

Channel typeTypical examplesHow you are usually toldCollection
Ticketing platforms used for store lotteries LivePocket and similar event-ticket systems Email, plus an application-history page in your account Usually in-store, on a fixed date range
Manufacturer direct / official stores Premium Bandai, Pokémon Center Online, Konami Style Account page. Email is not guaranteed Shipped
Large retail chains Electronics, supermarket and bookstore chains, plus their apps Email or in-app notice, chain by chain Mixed: some ship, many are store pickup only
Convenience stores and rental/game chains Konbini apps, used-media chains App notification, often winners only Store pickup

The practical consequence: there is no single inbox that tells you what you won. Anyone treating email as their source of truth will lose product they actually won.

3. Why silence means you lost

A large share of Japanese lotteries follow a convention stated in the fine print as “当選者のみ” — winners only will be contacted. There is no loss notification. There is no “better luck next time” email.

Rule of thumb

Write down the announced result date for every entry. If the date passes and nothing has arrived, treat it as a loss and close the entry. Waiting for a confirmation that is never coming is how people end up with a backlog of entries they can no longer reconstruct.

This is also why an entry log matters more than it sounds like it should. Once you are running twenty or thirty open applications across a dozen accounts, memory stops working, and the retailers are not going to reconcile it for you.

4. The trap that costs people the most

Here is the specific failure I hit myself, and it is the single most useful thing on this page.

Manufacturer-direct lotteries can send you nothing at all

On at least one major manufacturer-direct store, I received neither an entry confirmation nor a result notification — not for the win, and not for the loss. The only reliable source of truth was the lottery-status page inside my own account.

When I finally checked that page directly instead of my inbox, there were seven finished lotteries sitting there whose results I had never seen. All losses in that case — but the same blind spot would have silently swallowed a win.

So the working rule is: for manufacturer-direct stores, open the account’s lottery-status page on a schedule. Do not wait to be told. Email-based reconciliation is sufficient for ticketing platforms and most chains; it is not sufficient here.

A second, smaller version of the same lesson: on ticketing platforms, read your application-history page rather than opening each confirmation mail one at a time. The history page is faster, and it is often the only place that shows the cancellation deadline — which matters if you win something you can no longer collect.

5. Made-to-order is a different animal

Alongside lotteries, Japan uses 受注生産 (juchu seisan) — made-to-order runs. The order window is open for a fixed period, often very short, and everyone who orders gets one. There is no losing.

That sounds strictly better, and for availability it is. Two costs are easy to miss:

  • The window is brutally short. Twenty-four hours is a common format. If you find out the next day, it is over.
  • Delivery can be a year or more out. Some collaboration and made-to-order products ship in the following calendar year. Your money, or at least your card authorisation, may be committed long before the box exists.

Because supply is unlimited within the window, made-to-order items also behave differently on the secondary market than lottery items do. Scarcity is the thing being sold in a lottery; in a made-to-order run, it is not being sold at all.

6. What this means if you are outside Japan

Most of these windows are not realistically enterable from abroad, and it is worth being precise about why, because the reasons are different and only some of them can be worked around.

  • Account requirements. Many entries require a Japanese address and phone number on a verified account. Some also require prior purchase history or paid membership at that specific retailer.
  • In-store collection. A large fraction of chain lotteries are pickup-only at a named branch, inside a short window. No proxy service can fix a lottery that was never shippable.
  • Language and timing. Windows open and close on Japan time and are announced in Japanese, frequently with only a few days’ notice.
  • Social conditions. Some store lotteries require following or reposting from a specific Japanese social account, which effectively requires an account that looks lived-in.

The honest summary: the shippable, manufacturer-direct and online-chain windows are the ones a proxy arrangement can realistically serve. The store-pickup ones are structurally closed to you, and no amount of paying a service changes that.

If someone offers to “enter Japanese lotteries on your behalf” for a fee, the first question worth asking is which of the four channel types above they actually mean — because the answer determines whether they are selling you access or selling you a queue position that does not exist.
